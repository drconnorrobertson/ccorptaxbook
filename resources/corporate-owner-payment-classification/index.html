Corporate Owner Payments: Salary, Dividend, Loan, or Reimbursement?

A transfer from a C corporation to its owner needs an explanation that both sides of the transaction can follow. Start with what the company paid for, who received the benefit and which records support the payment. A generic owner draw account or a year-end journal entry does not answer those questions.

This guide helps assemble a payment file for professional review. It does not choose a tax category for an actual transfer. Keep the corporation's reporting separate from the shareholder's reporting, then reconcile the two through the responsible preparers.

Identify the underlying transaction before choosing the ledger account

For each transfer, record the legal payer and recipient, payment date, amount, business purpose and the obligation being paid. Distinguish a payment made directly to the owner from a company payment to a vendor for something the owner used. Identify any related entities so the preparer does not treat several businesses as one bank account.

Separate the tax rules from the bookkeeping description

The IRS explains that corporate officers generally are employees, with a limited exception for officers who perform no or minor services and receive no pay. Its paying yourself guidance also addresses officer compensation and shareholder loans. Review duties, compensation support and reporting with the payroll and tax advisers rather than assuming a dividend can replace payment for services.

For a C corporation, distributions from earnings and profits generally are dividends, and the corporation generally cannot deduct dividends. Other distribution treatment depends on the applicable facts and rules. The company's cash balance alone does not establish earnings and profits or the shareholder's result. IRS Publication 542 discusses corporate distributions and related issues. Ask the preparer to identify the analysis needed for the particular payment.

Below-market loans can create additional tax consequences. A debt review needs the terms and actual conduct, not simply a receivable or payable balance. Neither a signed note nor the word loan in an email is a complete conclusion about a transaction.

Build a ledger that preserves the direction of each payment

Keep a separate ledger for each corporation and shareholder. Use one row per underlying item or clearly identified component, with links to the supporting records in the approved document system. Include the payment reference, date, amount, purpose, proposed category, document status, reviewer and final treatment. Mark unresolved items as unresolved instead of filling a blank category with the preferred answer.

If a shareholder lends money to the corporation, the company may owe the shareholder. If the company advances money to the shareholder, the direction is reversed. Do not net the two balances without a supported reconciliation and professional review. An unexplained year-end net number can conceal different obligations, owners or periods.

For loan-related records, show opening principal, new advances, principal repayments, interest separately, and closing principal. Explain differences between that ledger, the bank activity and the general ledger. Record changes in terms and missed payments rather than continuing a schedule that no longer describes the actual arrangement.

Worked recordkeeping example: one transfer, several explanations

Fictional example only: a corporation transfers $12,000 to its shareholder. The internal note proposes $3,000 for business expenses, $4,000 as repayment of an earlier owner advance and $5,000 as a distribution. The three amounts add to $12,000, but matching arithmetic does not establish any of those categories.

The expense component needs its expense report, receipts and business-purpose review. The repayment component needs the original advance and the debt ledger, with any interest handled separately. The proposed distribution needs the corporate and shareholder analysis. If one component lacks support, flag that component; do not move it into another category just to complete the reconciliation.

The final payment file should show the bank transfer once and the proposed components separately, so the same $12,000 is not counted again as three additional cash payments. Preserve the final reviewer-approved breakdown and identify any correction required in the company records or related reporting.

Coordinate the corporate and shareholder preparers

  1. Reconcile the source records. Match bank transfers, vendor payments and payroll activity to the owner-payment ledger. Explain duplicates, reversals and timing differences.
  2. Resolve the category. Send the facts and supporting records to the responsible adviser. Keep questions about compensation, distributions and debt distinct.
  3. Identify the reporting work. Ask who handles payroll, information reporting, company accounts and the shareholder return. Record which periods are affected.
  4. Document corrections. An approved adjustment needs a reason, date and reviewer. A corrected ledger does not by itself show that all related filings have been corrected.
  5. Close the handoff. Provide both preparers the final schedule and unresolved-item list. Retain the explanation behind the result.

Questions to settle before taking more cash out

Forecast the owner's cash need and the corporation's operating needs before the next transfer. Review existing loan balances, planned compensation, distribution history and personal expenses already paid by the company. Ask what documentation and approval should be in place before payment, rather than relying on a cleanup at filing time.

Use the companion guides on shareholder loan records, owner compensation and benefits budgeting and company and shareholder tax modeling to prepare related questions. These reviews inform one another, but they do not turn every transfer into the same type of payment.

Common owner-payment questions

Can I call every payment to a C corporation owner a draw?

A generic draw label does not resolve the transaction. Identify the payer, recipient, purpose and supporting records, then have the preparer determine the appropriate accounting and tax treatment.

Does a loan agreement by itself settle shareholder loan treatment?

No. The agreement needs to be evaluated with the actual transaction, repayment ability, interest, payment history and other relevant facts. Keep principal and interest records separate and tell the adviser about missed or changed payments.

Can I reclassify an owner payment at year end to reduce tax?

A correcting entry should reflect the supported facts and applicable treatment. Do not invent a transaction or assume changing a ledger label changes the underlying payment. Have the preparers assess any payroll, information reporting and return corrections.

What should both preparers receive?

Provide a reconciled owner-payment ledger, supporting documents, proposed treatment, unresolved questions and the final approved adjustments. Identify the legal entity, shareholder, dates and periods so the corporate and individual reporting can be coordinated.
