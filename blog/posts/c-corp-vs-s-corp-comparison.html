C Corp vs S Corp: Compare Company Tax and Owner Cash

Published June 29, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026 · AE Tax Advisors

A C corporation's tax rate and an owner's personal tax rate answer different questions. A useful C-corp-versus-S-corp comparison follows money through the company and the owner, identifies the same business assumptions in both alternatives, and includes the cost and consequences of changing the existing arrangement.

This is a preparation guide for an adviser-led comparison. The fictional calculation below explains why a company-level rate alone cannot determine the owner's result. It does not recommend an election or estimate a reader's tax.

Compare where income is taxed

IRS Publication 542, Corporations, describes the general federal corporate income-tax calculation using taxable income and a 21% rate. That company-level calculation is not a combined company-and-shareholder tax result. Payments to an owner must be classified and modeled separately, and retaining money in the company is different from delivering spendable cash to the owner.

The IRS S-corporation overview explains that S corporations generally pass income, losses, deductions and credits through to shareholders. It also identifies entity-level taxes on certain built-in gains and passive income. Avoid the blanket statement that an S corporation can never owe federal income tax.

Publication 550's S-corporation discussion notes that current-year tax items can be taxed to a shareholder whether or not cash is distributed. Model the owner's tax-funding need separately from the business's decision to keep cash for operations.

A fictional illustration of two tax layers

Assume an invented C corporation has $100,000 of taxable income after all deductions included in this simplified example. Assume the general 21% federal corporate rate, no credits or other company taxes, and distribution of the entire remaining amount as a dividend. For illustration only, assume the shareholder's tax on that dividend is a flat 20%; this is an input to the example, not a rate assigned to every shareholder.

  1. Company federal income tax: $100,000 × 21% = $21,000.
  2. Cash remaining for the assumed dividend: $100,000 − $21,000 = $79,000.
  3. Assumed shareholder dividend tax: $79,000 × 20% = $15,800.
  4. Cash remaining after those two assumed taxes: $79,000 − $15,800 = $63,200.

The two assumed taxes total $36,800, or 36.8% of the starting taxable income. State taxes, net investment income tax, payroll, credits, other shareholder facts and timing differences are outside this illustration. It does not establish an S-corporation result or a winner. If the company retains the $79,000 instead, that is company cash, not the same outcome as $63,200 available to the owner.

Use the same owner-pay assumptions

Start with the work the owner performs, the proposed compensation and the records supporting it. Do not treat an arbitrary split between salary and distributions as an established tax treatment. The IRS compensation and medical-insurance guidance explains reasonable compensation for S-corporation shareholder-employees and the potential reclassification of payments as wages.

Have the adviser and payroll provider apply the rules and thresholds for the actual year. Record employer payroll cost, employee withholding and owner take-home cash separately. A comparison that includes payroll in one column but omits it from the other cannot establish a meaningful difference.

Review benefits by benefit and ownership facts

Publication 15-B, Employer's Tax Guide to Fringe Benefits, explains exclusions and special treatment for more-than-2% S-corporation shareholders. C-corporation status does not make every proposed benefit tax-free. S-corporation health-insurance reporting also cannot be reduced to the claim that an owner receives no relevant deduction; the IRS medical-insurance guidance describes conditions that need review.

List the benefit, eligible participants, ownership, plan terms, company cost, payroll reporting and individual-return treatment. Ask which conditions remain unconfirmed. Compare the same proposed benefit package rather than assigning a generic annual saving to the entity label.

Keep ownership, losses and exit assumptions explicit

Confirm S-election eligibility, ownership and effective dates before modeling S status. If losses are projected, ask which owner-level limits and carryforwards apply. A pass-through loss shown in a projection is not proof that it can offset the owner's other income.

For a possible sale, distinguish a stock sale from an asset sale and identify the actual shareholder and stock history. Section 1202 of the U.S. Code contains date-dependent rules and conditions for qualified small business stock. C-corporation status alone does not establish eligibility, a particular exclusion amount or a tax-free exit. Have the adviser document the applicable law, acquisition and issuance dates, holding period, business activity and other requirements instead of assuming a single rule applies to every stock lot.

Prepare a comparison workpaper

  • Common business baseline: period, revenue and expense assumptions, owner services, staffing and proposed investment.
  • Company column: taxable-income adjustments, owner compensation, benefits, company taxes, debt payments and cash retained.
  • Owner column: wages, distributions, pass-through items, owner taxes and after-tax cash available.
  • Timing: dates of tax payments, withdrawals and planned business spending.
  • Changing the structure: transaction steps, professional costs and tax consequences identified for this existing business.
  • Uncertainty: assumptions, missing records and the person responsible for resolving each question.

Run the alternatives with the same stated needs: the owner's required cash, the business's operating reserve and the proposed use of retained money. If an assumption changes, show both versions. For supporting records, use the corporate cash business-purpose file and owner-payment classification guide. Ask the adviser to explain the combined result and its limitations before deciding on an election or conversion.
