C Corporation Deferred Compensation: Review the Promise, Tax Timing and Cash

July 21, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · AE Tax Advisors

Nonqualified deferred compensation exchanges current pay for a contractual promise of later payment. For a C corporation, evaluate both sides: the employee’s tax and payment rights, and the employer’s deduction timing, liquidity and liability. More cash left in the company is not necessarily more spendable wealth for the employee.

NQDC is not a C corporation-only benefit

Section 409A does not define NQDC as a C-corporation-only product. Sponsor and recipient status affect the analysis. An owner’s label, high salary or participation in a qualified retirement plan does not independently establish a compliant arrangement.

Employee coverage also requires review. ERISA section 201(2) specifies unfunded and select-management-or-highly-compensated-group conditions for its participation-and-vesting exception. Do not replace that analysis with “no employee rules.” Determine whether ERISA applies and what eligibility, reporting and other obligations remain.

Election and payment terms need review before compensation is earned

Section 409A generally requires an elective deferral decision before the year of services, with specified exceptions. Payments must fit permitted events or schedules, and acceleration is restricted. First-year eligibility and performance-based compensation rules have conditions; a late signature is not a universal cure.

Subsequent-election requirements can include a 12-month effectiveness delay and a five-year payment deferral measured from the original payment date, with applicable exceptions and notice timing. They are not simply a rule to wait five years after changing a date. Certain publicly traded company specified employees face a six-month separation-payment delay.

A relevant failure can trigger inclusion of affected vested deferred compensation, a 20% additional federal tax and an interest-based charge under section 409A. Have the plan’s written terms and actual operation reviewed together.

Employee income and the corporate deduction have separate tests

Section 404(a)(5) generally ties the employer’s deduction for the relevant nonqualified arrangement to employee income inclusion, subject to deductibility and other conditions. An unpaid promise or bookkeeping accrual is not automatically a current deduction. A later deduction may also have different cash value if the company has insufficient taxable income.

The IRS NQDC Audit Technique Guide (March 2024) discusses constructive receipt, economic benefit and other timing issues. An agreement alone does not guarantee deferred employee income. The guide is examination background, not an official pronouncement of law; apply the current statutes and relevant regulations to the actual plan.

Payroll timing may precede the payout

Section 3121(v)(2) generally takes the relevant deferred amount into account for FICA at the later of performance of services or lapse of substantial forfeiture risk, with additional rules. That can precede income-tax payment timing. Record payroll inclusion separately rather than assuming every tax waits until retirement.

Original fictional company-cash review: three alternatives

Assume an invented corporation has $180,000 of income and matching cash before this compensation decision, no beginning cash and no other cash flows. In each alternative, the total gross compensation obligation is $180,000. Current payments are assumed fully deductible; unpaid deferred amounts are assumed not deductible now. Use the 21% regular corporate rate under section 11.

The arrangements are assumed valid solely to isolate this cash arithmetic. No investment return, credited earnings, state tax, employment tax, employee income tax, fees, credits or other adjustments are modeled. Employee net payout and future corporate deduction value remain unresolved. This is not a compliant plan design or a choice recommendation.

In the full-deferral case, current company tax is $180,000 × 21% = $37,800. The remaining cash is $142,200, not $180,000. The gross promise is still $180,000, leaving $37,800 to source elsewhere before payment if there are no other changes. In the half-deferral case, $180,000 − $90,000 pay − $18,900 tax = $71,100 cash against a $90,000 unpaid promise.

These differences measure a simplified funding need, not a tax saving, legal funding requirement or actual insolvency. Future business cash, tax deductions and asset returns could change available resources, but none is assumed here. Do not add a hypothetical future deduction as though it were cash already available to invest.

Informal assets are not protected employee cash

The IRS guide explains the importance of the employer’s general creditors in an unfunded arrangement, including a rabbi trust. A corporate investment account is not automatically a segregated employee-owned retirement account. Creditor exposure and restrictions on access remain part of the decision.

Match the actual assets and their accessible cash values to the payment schedule. If insurance is considered, review the contract, costs and available values; a death-benefit face amount is not cash available for scheduled compensation. An investment or insurance illustration does not establish that the employer can pay every promised benefit.

Keep tax timing apart from accounting and sale value

A delayed tax deduction is not evidence that EBITDA increases. Reconcile the book expense, compensation liability, cash and the buyer’s treatment of obligations before claiming a higher sale price. The unpaid promise does not disappear because ownership changes.

Likewise, compensation and QSBS stock-sale gain are separate items. Do not treat an eligible stock-gain exclusion as an exemption for deferred wages. A future personal tax rate, sale value or ability to pay needs supported assumptions rather than a guaranteed savings headline.

Prepare one plan, payroll and payment record

Download a blank NQDC review record (CSV). Preserve the plan and election dates, services covered, vesting, payment terms, income and deduction decisions, payroll review, creditor exposure and available resources. Leave unknown tax conclusions unresolved.

Continue with the company and owner cash comparison and payment-classification guide. Neither a preferred bracket nor a large deferral amount is enough to choose or approve a plan.
