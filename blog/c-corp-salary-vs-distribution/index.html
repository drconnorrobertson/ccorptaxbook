C Corporation Salary vs. Distributions: An Evidence Guide

Separate the payment purpose, supporting evidence and tax analysis.

A payment to a C corporation owner needs a supported classification before anyone compares its tax cost. Start by identifying whether the payment relates to work, ownership, a documented loan or another transaction. The practical question is what the records support, rather than which label produces the most attractive spreadsheet result.

Start with the services the officer actually performs

The IRS paying-yourself guidance says corporate officers are generally employees, with a limited exception for an officer who performs no or only minor services and has no right to pay. It also connects officer wages to the duties performed. A shareholder title alone does not describe the employee role.

For your compensation file, describe responsibilities, time devoted to each role, authority, business size and the period covered. Save the source and date of any comparable-pay information. A single industry average without a role comparison gives the reviewer little basis for assessing your particular facts. Use the owner compensation field guide to organize the broader review.

Do not assume a large salary resolves every distribution question

IRS Publication 542, under Unreasonable salaries, explains that excessive shareholder-employee pay may be treated as a distribution. Its distribution discussion also ties dividend character to current or accumulated earnings and profits, with other amounts potentially reducing stock basis and then producing gain. Those rules require analysis beyond the amount in the bank.

Ask the adviser to show which corporate records support the proposed wage and distribution treatment. Keep the earnings-and-profits workpaper and shareholder basis information distinct from the payroll file. This article does not prescribe a salary percentage, dividend amount or universal allocation between the two.

Build two cash views before comparing alternatives

Use one column for the corporation and another for the shareholder. In the corporate column record the proposed payment, related payroll costs, applicable tax assumptions and remaining operating cash. In the shareholder column record gross receipts, withholding or other modeled tax amounts, and cash available after those amounts. Label each rate and deduction assumption for the adviser to confirm.

A hypothetical $40,000 cash payment is a useful amount to trace, but it does not establish a $40,000 deduction, a reasonable salary or the shareholder's net proceeds. Model the actual classification and applicable treatment instead of assuming every dollar paid has the same consequence. Keep working-capital needs visible while the tax treatment is reviewed.

Keep loans and reimbursements out of a catch-all distribution line

The IRS paying-yourself page describes evidence associated with an arm's-length officer loan, including documented terms and repayment obligations. Calling a transfer a loan in the ledger does not supply that evidence. Keep the agreement, interest terms, repayment schedule and actual payment history together for review.

Likewise, put a claimed expense reimbursement in its own row with the underlying business-purpose record and approval. This is a recordkeeping method, not a conclusion that a particular reimbursement qualifies for any exclusion. Ask the reviewer to identify missing evidence and the treatment supported by the facts.

Use a payment-classification file at year end

Download the payment-classification worksheet. For each transfer record its date, recipient, amount, stated purpose, supporting document, proposed category and unresolved questions. Reconcile the total to the relevant bank and ledger records, then compare reporting decisions with the approved classification.

Example: a shareholder received monthly payroll and a separate year-end transfer described only as “owner pay.” Keep that transfer unresolved until the reviewer examines the services, authorizations and distribution analysis. Renaming it in bookkeeping software is not the same as resolving its character.

Questions to take to the tax adviser

  • Which facts support compensation for services, and what additional comparable-pay evidence is needed?
  • Who will prepare the earnings-and-profits and shareholder basis analysis for any distribution?
  • Do the corporate and shareholder models use the same transaction dates and consistent assumptions?
  • Which approvals, payroll records and information returns follow from the final treatment?
